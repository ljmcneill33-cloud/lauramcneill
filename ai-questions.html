10 Questions to Ask Before Bringing AI Into Your Course

A practical checklist for faculty, instructional designers and academic leaders. Work through it before you redesign, restrict or embrace AI — and you’ll make decisions you can explain to students and stand behind.

Get support

  1. What should students be able to do without AI?

    Start with your learning outcomes, not the tools. Some skills must be practiced unaided to be learned at all; others can be extended or accelerated with help.

    Ask yourself: Which outcomes would be hollowed out if AI did the work?

  2. Where could AI genuinely support learning?

    AI can give feedback on drafts, generate practice questions, explain concepts another way or model expert thinking. Look for places where it adds practice, not shortcuts.

    Ask yourself: Where do my students get stuck, and could AI help them get unstuck without doing the thinking for them?

  3. What is my policy — and can I explain why?

    Students follow policies they understand. A clear rationale, tied to your discipline and outcomes, matters more than whether the answer is “yes,” “no” or “it depends.”

    Ask yourself: Could a student explain my AI policy, and the reason for it, in two sentences?

  4. Does the policy change by assignment?

    One rule rarely fits a whole course. Labeling each assignment — AI not allowed, allowed with disclosure, or encouraged — removes guesswork.

    Ask yourself: Have I marked AI expectations on every major assignment?

  5. Which assessments are most vulnerable?

    Generic prompts with a single polished product are easiest to outsource. Process work, personal connection, local context and in-class components are more resilient.

    Ask yourself: Which assignments could be completed convincingly by AI in five minutes?

  6. How will students show their thinking?

    Drafts, reflections, annotated sources, oral check-ins and revision histories make learning visible — and make the final product only part of the story.

    Ask yourself: Where in this assignment does a student’s reasoning become visible to me?

  7. Is access equitable?

    Not every student has the same devices, paid tools or prior experience. If AI use is expected, make sure everyone has a fair, supported way in.

    Ask yourself: If I require or allow AI, can every student access a suitable tool at no cost?

  8. What about privacy and data?

    Student work and personal information shouldn’t be fed into tools without understanding where it goes. Check your institution’s approved tools and data guidance.

    Ask yourself: Do I know what happens to anything my students (or I) put into this tool?

  9. How will I teach students to use AI well?

    Students need guidance on prompting, checking accuracy, citing use and recognizing bias — skills that don’t develop on their own.

    Ask yourself: Where in the course do I model and practice responsible AI use?

  10. How will I know if it’s working?

    Treat AI changes like any course redesign: gather evidence. Compare outcomes, ask students what helped, and adjust next term.

    Ask yourself: What will I look at at the end of the term to decide whether to keep, change or drop this approach?
